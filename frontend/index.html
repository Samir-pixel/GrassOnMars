              Mission concept

              Grass on Mars I

              A crewed surface campaign at Jezero crater: four astronauts, about 500 sols (three-quarters of a Mars year) of science on foot, oxygen made from the air, and the first greenhouse on another planet. Every number below is either measured by a NASA mission or derived from NASA design studies, and the walks are planned on real terrain by the same engine as the Marswalk tab.

              4 crew~500 sols on the surface Jezero crater, 18.44°N 77.45°EConjunction-class trajectory
              01

              Why this mission

              The question, the answer we propose, and why Jezero.

              The question

              Was Mars ever alive, and can people live and work there safely? Rovers move a few hundred metres a day and choose samples slowly. Human geologists on foot can cover kilometres, read an outcrop in minutes and adapt the plan on the spot.

              Why Jezero

              An ancient lake with a river delta, where fine mud and carbonates can preserve signs of past life. Perseverance has mapped it in detail and left a sample depot. In our ranking Jezero is #1 for astrobiology and fully walkable on real terrain.

              The honest trade-off

              Jezero has no shallow ice. Arcadia Planitia scores higher for a long-term base because ice lies about a metre down. Grass on Mars I chooses science first and plans water from hydrated minerals plus recycling; a later base can move north.

              How Marswalk decides

              How it works NASA data Calculations Tests Open data Limits
              1

              Collect NASA data

              14 sources, 13 of them NASA, with data from 5 missions (Mars Global Surveyor, Mars Odyssey, MRO, Curiosity, Perseverance): MOLA and CTX elevation, HiRISE and CTX images, THEMIS infrared, CRISM minerals, SHARAD radar, MEDA weather, rover waypoints, RAD radiation, MOXIE, plus IAU place names, Mars24 time and the Mars Fact Sheet.

              Spacecraft around Mars and rovers on the ground measure the height of the land, where ice hides, how strong sunlight is and how much radiation there is.

              2

              Calculate

              8 formulas: barometric pressure, Earth-equivalent altitude, sunlight by distance and latitude, Tobler walking speed, oxygen reserve, radiation dose, weighted index, Mars24 clock.

              We turn the measurements into simple numbers: how thick the air is, how fast you can walk, how much air the suit has left.

              3

              Decide and check

              Rank sites with explicit weights and NASA exploration limits; plan routes with A* on real slopes; GO only if oxygen, daylight and dust rules pass.

              We add everything up to pick the best place, then test every walk so the crew always has enough air to come home before dark.

              Problem

              Choices with no rescue nearby

              The first crews on Mars will choose where to work and how to move on foot with no rescue nearby. Today the data for these choices sits in separate mission archives.

              Answer

              Calculations, step by step

              Each card shows a formula, the NASA numbers that go into it and the answer. Move the sliders, or press “Explain simply” and Zyro will say it in plain words.

              Where the index inputs come from

              S = Σ wᵢ · xᵢ, xᵢ ∈ [0, 1]

              All 5 inputs are derived from NASA data: water class from SHARAD/SWIM/CRISM/Phoenix/InSight, sunlight cos(lat), pressure from MOLA elevation, terrain 1 − p₉₀/8° from a 10 km MOLA slope window, and 5 binary NASA astrobiology observables.

              Route cost

              C = Σ D · (w_d + w_s·S + w_r·T + w_h·R + w_u·U − w_v·V)

              A* on the region’s elevation grid (CTX in Jezero, MOLA elsewhere), on the backend. D is 3D ground distance in metres; S slope, T roughness, R hazard score, U data uncertainty, V closeness to a science target, each 0 to 1. NoData cells and slopes above your limit are never crossed; the lowest-risk mode also forbids HIGH-hazard cells, and science value counts only on LOW-hazard ground.

              Scientific method and tests

              1. QuestionWhere can a crew live and work safely, and can they walk to the best science and back?
              2. HypothesisSites with shallow ice, good sunlight and thicker air rank highest for a base; ancient lake sites like Jezero win when science matters most.
              3. DataMOLA elevation and slopes, SHARAD and HiRISE ice evidence, CRISM minerals, the CTX elevation model of Jezero, MEDA, REMS and Viking weather, RAD radiation.
              4. ModelsWeighted index, barometric pressure, terrain hazard index, A* routing on real slopes, Tobler walking speed slowed for a suit.
              5. TestsPressure model checked against MEDA; ranking stress-tested with 1,000 random trials; every route checked for oxygen and daylight.
              6. ResultArcadia leads for a greenhouse base; Jezero leads for astrobiology and is walkable from the landing site on real terrain.

              Model check against a real measurement

              Model at Jezero (−2.6 km)774 Pa
              Perseverance MEDA, sol 100 (NASA PDS)754–771 Pa
              Differenceunder 3%

              The model sits just above what the rover measured over that sol. One sol is a check, not a calibration: surface pressure on Mars rises and falls by roughly a quarter to a third over a year as CO₂ freezes onto and returns from the polar caps.

              We compared our formula with real readings from the Perseverance rover. They differ by less than 3%, far less than the air on Mars changes between seasons.

              Ranking stress test

              1,000 random trials change every weight by up to ±50% and add measurement uncertainty. The leader stays #1 in most trials; the full result is on the Compare tab.

              We shake the numbers a thousand times to see if the winner changes. If it keeps winning, we trust the result more.

              Why it matters on Earth and in Tajikistan

              On Earth

              The same engine plans safe walks on Earth: swap the Mars elevation for NASA’s free global elevation data, and slope, daylight and supply checks help mountain rescue teams, geology field trips and trekkers.

              The Pamirs, our Mars analog

              A high, cold, dry plateau around 4,000 m. The air there is about 62 kPa, Dushanbe (≈800 m) about 92 kPa, Jezero about 0.77 kPa: over 100 times thinner than in Dushanbe.

              Ice we depend on

              Tajikistan’s glaciers, including the Fedchenko Glacier (about 77 km long), feed the Vakhsh and Panj, which form the Amu Darya. The orbital imaging and radar ideas used to find ice on Mars also help track glaciers from space.

              For our schools

              A friendly guide who explains every number in plain words, and open data a class can check. Built in Dushanbe for students anywhere.

              Limits of this prototype

              • Routes use every third cell of the 20 m CTX elevation model. The backend computes slopes, hazard and routes on that 60 m sample, so ledges and boulders smaller than that are invisible to the planner, and routes follow 8 grid directions. Route weights are planning preferences, not validated safety thresholds. If the backend is offline, route planning stops rather than falling back to synthetic terrain.
              • Site comparison parameters are derived from orbital and landed NASA records. Elevations and 10 km 90th-percentile slopes come directly from MGS MOLA DEM (463 m); water class and the 5-criterion science count summarize published detections from SHARAD, SWIM, CRISM, TES, Phoenix, InSight and the rovers.
              • Surface weather comes from archived NASA PDS MEDA tables of a few loaded sols, at the rover’s position on each sol; it is not current weather, not a forecast, and it is not extended across the crater. The Environment tab lists every source and whether it is loaded: THEMIS is a relative infrared mosaic, not a temperature; dust and radiation series are not loaded. Baseline dust τ = 0.45 is the MEDA SkyCam clear-season mean, and the slider tests storm scenarios.
              • Outside Jezero the terrain is the 463 m MGS MOLA elevation model. Each other region is a 1° window around a NASA landing point or the site centre; destinations there are IAU-named features. Slopes, hazard classes and routes are regional estimates: nothing smaller than a cell is resolved, and the clock, daylight window and MEDA weather stay Jezero values.
              • The landing check is a planning screen, not a safety guarantee. It applies the limits of NASA’s 2015 Human Landing Sites workshop (latitude within ±50°, elevation below +2 km, slope up to 10°) and requires a LOW hazard cell with no HIGH or UNKNOWN neighbour. Rock abundance, dust thickness and bearing strength are not checked: no dataset for them is loaded.
              • All six Jezero destinations are exact NASA Perseverance rover waypoints (sols 201–1148) inside the CTX elevation grid.
              • The 3D view and the postcard show relief colours computed from the elevation model, not a photograph. Heights in 3D are stretched by the exaggeration slider.
              • Globe texture is a 4K color map and relief normal map from the Celestia project, built from USGS Viking and Mars Global Surveyor data. Photos come from NASA Photojournal.
